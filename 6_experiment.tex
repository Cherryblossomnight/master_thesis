\chapter{Experiments}
In this chapter, we will demonstrate the effects of the proposed hybrid impedance--admittance control framework. Hydrus Xi is selected as the primary experimental platform, while DRAGON is used as an extension platform to demonstrate the cross-platform feasibility of the proposed framework.   The following sections present the experimental platforms, controller calibration and component-level validation, comparative surface sliding experiments on Hydrus Xi, and cross-platform feasibility demonstration on DRAGON.

\section{Experimental Platforms}

\subsection{Hydrus Xi: Primary Experimental Platform}
The underactuated multi-link aerial robot Hydrus Xi used in the experiments is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:robot_hydrus}. The robot consists of four serial links connected by three rotational joints. All joint axes are parallel, so that the deformation is constrained in a plane. Each link is equipped with a gimbal module with a propeller, which enables thrust vectoring through a 1-DoF yaw rotation mechanism. The platform is equipped with an UP-Board single-board computer featuring an Intel Atom x5-Z8350 CPU for onboard computation.

Due to this robotic-arm-like mechanism, DRAGON can deform in three-dimensional space and achieve more complex configurations than Hydrus Xi. Each link is equipped with an independent gimbal module with two ducted rotors. The gimbal module provides 2-DoF motion to realize more flexible thrust vectoring under complex deformations. \section{Controller Calibration and Component-Level Validation on Hydrus Xi}
\subsection{Parameter-Selection Principles}
\label{sec:parameter_selection}


In this work, the underactuated multi-link aerial robot Hydrus Xi was used as the primary experimental platform, so its controller parameters were selected according to the following practical considerations. These parameters were tuned during preliminary experiments before conducting the main surface sliding experiments.

Since Hydrus Xi is an underactuated system, the rotational and translational parameters need to be tuned in a cascaded manner, with the rotational parameters tuned first, followed by the translational parameters. According to the principle mentioned in Sec.~\ref{sec:imp_param_tuning}, the PD parameters were considered first. To achieve fast convergence to the desired orientation, $\tilde{c}_d$ in the roll and pitch axes was set to a relatively large value. Meanwhile, to avoid oscillation, the damping ratio $\zeta$ in these two axes was tuned to approximately $1.2$--$1.4$. The remaining parameters were then adjusted according to the experimental conditions.

Next, the mass ratio $\mu$ in each direction needs to be taken into account. To reduce the influence of external torques on the orientation, the mass ratio $\mu$ in these directions was set relatively large, typically around $5$--$10$ in our experiments. For the translational directions, the selection depends on the task requirements. In the surface sliding task, contact is usually required in one direction while sliding occurs in the other two directions, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:hybrid_controller}. In the sliding directions, it is important to resist friction-induced disturbances. Therefore, $\mu$ is set larger than $1$, typically around $0.3$--$1$ in our experiments. In the contact direction, only admittance behavior is required, while the impedance characteristic is not introduced. Therefore, $\mu$ is set exactly to $1$.

The admittance parameters were also selected based on the following principles.

The admittance stiffness $k_a$ was mainly chosen according to the task requirements. If a large geometric variation is expected, a relatively small stiffness should be selected, and vice versa. Meanwhile, a larger stiffness results in less obvious deformation, whereas a smaller stiffness may introduce significant configuration variations that could lead to instability. Therefore, the admittance stiffness needs to be appropriately tuned according to the actual task conditions.

The admittance mass $m_a$ was selected mainly according to the required response speed. A larger admittance mass results in a slower response, which may lead to insufficient adaptation to relatively fast geometric variations. On the other hand, a smaller admittance mass results in a faster response, which may introduce instability since the configuration may change too rapidly. Overall, the admittance mass also needs to be appropriately tuned according to the task requirements.

Finally, the admittance damping $c_a$ was usually selected together with $m_a$ and $k_a$. The damping ratio $\zeta=\frac{c_a}{2\sqrt{m_a k_a}}$ was generally set slightly below $1$ to obtain a response close to critical damping.


\subsection{Free-Flight Baseline Calibration and Validation}
\label{sec:free_flight}

According to the parameter tuning principle mentioned in Sec.~\ref{sec:imp_param_tuning}, the PD parameters are first tuned under free flight conditions before introducing contact interaction. Note that at this stage, the mass ratio $\mu$ in all directions is set to $1$, so that no additional impedance characteristic is introduced. Meanwhile, the admittance controller is deactivated. Therefore, the controller in this experiment can be regarded as the baseline PD controller.

The parameters obtained from the free flight tuning are summarized in Table.~\ref{tb:params1}.

\end{align}
\end{subequations}

After the free flight tuning process, these tuned parameters were used as the basic controller parameters for the following contact experiments.

\subsection{Impedance Response under External Force}
\label{sec:impedance_parameter}

In this experiment, the effect of the mass ratio $\mu$ will be investigated. According to Sec.~\ref{sec:imp_param_tuning}, the mass ratio $\mu$ can reshape the behavior of the aerial robot. In particular, $\mu<1$ provides a more rigid response to external disturbances, whereas $\mu>1$ provides a softer response.

As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:imp_drag}, a rope was used to horizontally drag Hydrus Xi and generate an external force along the $x$ axis. Pulling the rope along the positive direction of the $x$ axis can be regarded as applying a positive external force along this direction. The other controller parameters were kept the same as those used in the free flight experiment, as listed in Table~\ref{tb:params1}. Three different mass ratios, $\mu=2$, $1$, and $0.5$, were tested, corresponding to the soft, normal, and rigid cases, respectively.

Consequently, the results demonstrate that by varying the mass ratio $\mu$, the aerial robot can change its response to the external force. Therefore, an appropriate mass ratio can be selected according to the experimental conditions and task requirements.

\section{Comparative Surface-Sliding Evaluation on Hydrus Xi}
\subsection{Experimental Setup}

In the hybrid control experiment, we used Hydrus Xi as the experimental platform. The basic PD parameters were kept the same as those listed in Table.~\ref{tb:params1}. Based on the experimental results demonstrated in Sec.~\ref{sec:impedance_parameter}, the mass ratio $\mu$ in each direction was selected according to the experimental requirements. For the orientation, $\mu$ was chosen to be sufficiently large to resist external torques generated during contact and sliding. For the sliding directions, especially the $y$ axis, $\mu$ was selected to be larger than $1$ to resist external disturbances. Along the contact direction, namely the $x$ axis, the mass ratio $\mu$ was set to $1$. The resulting parameters are summarized in Table.~\ref{tb:params4}. Note that the diagonal elements of the linear mass ratio matrix $m\boldsymbol{M}_d^{-1}$ and the inertia ratio matrix $\boldsymbol{I}\boldsymbol{I}_d^{-1}$ represent the mass ratios $\mu$ in the corresponding translational and rotational directions, respectively. Meanwhile, the admittance controller was activated only along the $x$ axis, whose parameters are also shown in Table.~\ref{tb:params4}. The reference external force used in the admittance controller was set to $f_{ref,x}=0$. The parameters used for external wrench estimation are also summarized in the same table.

Throughout the experiment, a constant yaw angle was maintained by keeping link 4 aligned with the $x$ axis of $\{W\}$ in order to reduce the external torque applied to the CoG. Specifically, we defined the configuration angle $\theta$ as the sole variable determining the configuration in the admittance controller, and then computed the corresponding joint angles analytically through inverse kinematics. The joint angles were determined as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:joint angles}
 \begin{bmatrix}
    q_1 \\
    q_2 \\
    q_3
    \end{bmatrix} 
    =
    \begin{bmatrix}
    \frac{\pi}{2}-\theta \\
    2\theta \\
    -\theta
    \end{bmatrix},
\end{equation}
where $\theta$ is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:admittance_drag}.


The experimental environment is illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:exp_scene}. A vertical board was placed at a known location within the testing area. The board's surface provided sufficient friction for stable contact while still allowing smooth sliding of the end-effector (marker pen). The surface was intentionally inclined by approximately $20^\circ$ with respect to the $y$ axis, and the inclination angle was kept unknown to the robot. Although the surface is planar, its unknown inclination introduces unmodeled geometric variations that serve a similar role to curved surfaces in this study. The friction coefficient was also unknown. Consequently, both the contact forces and the surface geometry remained unmodeled in this experiment, providing suitable conditions for evaluating the robot's adaptive sliding capability.

\subsection{Comparative Experiment Design}

For the comparative experiments, the reference trajectory of the multi-link aerial robot was defined on a plane perpendicular to the $x$ axis. The robot was commanded to follow the predefined circular trajectory with a radius of $0.30\,\mathrm{m}$ and a period of $14\,\mathrm{s}$ within this plane. Due to the board's unknown inclination angle, the desired circular trajectory resulted in approximately $0.15\,\mathrm{m}$ of geometric variation along the $x$ axis during one cycle, introducing measurable geometry-induced deviations during contact. This geometric variation was not incorporated into the reference trajectory generation or controller design. Therefore, the robot was required to adapt to this geometric variation online during contact.

To evaluate the proposed hybrid control framework, comparative experiments were conducted among four controllers: the baseline PD controller, the impedance controller, the admittance controller, and the proposed hybrid impedance--admittance controller. The baseline PD controller corresponds to the reduced form of the impedance controller, so the PD gains were manually tuned to provide stable tracking performance in free flight, as described in Sec.~\ref{sec:free_flight}. In the hybrid controller, impedance control was applied along the $y$ and $z$ axes to improve sliding resilience, while admittance control was applied along the $x$ axis to enable adaptive surface interaction. Note that each control method is executed independently along its respective axes. To demonstrate the necessity of combining impedance control and admittance control, we performed experiments using an impedance-only controller (without admittance-based joint adaptation) and an admittance-only controller (where the CoG state was regulated solely by the baseline PD controller).

The CoG and end effector trajectories show similar trends. In contrast, the admittance-only and baseline PD controllers resulted in noticeably smaller and more distorted trajectories.


Fig.~\ref{fig:admittance_exp} further illustrates how the multi-link aerial robot deformed to accommodate surface geometric variations during contact under the hybrid controller. \subsection{Sliding-Direction Tracking and Disturbance Rejection}

Fig.~\ref{fig:impedance_plot} presents the CoG trajectories together with the reference path under the four controllers. Note that during the sliding task, the $y$ and $z$ axes corresponded to the sliding directions, where friction-induced disturbances were dominant. Although Fig.~\ref{fig:impedance_plot} illustrates the overall trajectories of the four controllers, their performance differences become more evident in the tracking error distributions shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:impedance_box}. The figure shows the distributions of CoG tracking errors along the $y$ and $z$ axes, where smaller tracking errors indicate better trajectory tracking performance. In particular, the median tracking errors along the $y$ axis were approximately $0.134\,\mathrm{m}$ for the PD controller, $0.079\,\mathrm{m}$ for the impedance-only controller, $0.105\,\mathrm{m}$ for the admittance-only controller, and $0.083\,\mathrm{m}$ for the hybrid controller. 

The impedance-only controller achieved the smallest tracking error. The results showed the importance of the mass ratio $\mu$ in regulating the response to external disturbances during contact experiments. The hybrid controller achieved similar performance to the impedance-only controller, indicating that the introduction of admittance control in the contact direction did not significantly affect the impedance behavior in the sliding directions.

This part of the experiments shows that along the sliding directions, impedance control provided a resilient response to frictional disturbances, enabling more accurate sliding without requiring explicit friction values while still approximately following the desired trajectory.

\subsection{Contact-Direction Adaptation and Interaction Force}

Fig.~\ref{fig:admittance_plot} presents the CoG position and estimated external force along the $x$ axis. As further quantified in Fig.~\ref{fig:admittance_box}, the PD and impedance-only controllers showed relatively larger variations in the CoG position, with interquartile ranges of $0.133\,\mathrm{m}$ and $0.137\,\mathrm{m}$, respectively. In contrast, the interquartile ranges were reduced to $0.088\,\mathrm{m}$ and $0.084\,\mathrm{m}$ for the admittance-only and hybrid controllers, respectively. A smaller interquartile range indicates that the CoG position was less affected by the geometric variations introduced by the inclined board.

Fig.~\ref{fig:admittance_box} also shows the distributions of the estimated external force along the $x$ axis. Here, the median value reflects the overall influence of the external force on the CoG during the sliding process. A median value closer to zero indicates that the CoG was less affected by the external force induced by the surface geometric variations. The median external forces were approximately $-1.190\,\mathrm{N}$ for the PD controller, $-0.453\,\mathrm{N}$ for the impedance-only controller, $-0.712\,\mathrm{N}$ for the admittance-only controller, and $-0.328\,\mathrm{N}$ for the hybrid controller.  Among the four cases, the hybrid controller had the median force closest to zero, indicating the smallest overall influence of the surface interaction on the CoG.

  

The admittance-only and hybrid controllers exhibited similar reductions in CoG position variations, indicating that the configuration adaptation introduced by admittance control reduced the influence of surface geometric variations transmitted to the CoG. Meanwhile, the hybrid controller achieved the median external force closest to zero while maintaining this adaptation behavior. These results demonstrate that admittance control enabled the multi-link structure to deform in response to surface geometric variations, thereby reducing their influence on the CoG, while the hybrid controller combined this adaptation with improved contact force behavior.

\subsection{Compare on All Directions}
We use the data shown in Table~\ref{tb:hybrid} to comprehensively compare the resilience and adaptability of the four controllers. The best and second-best results for each metric are highlighted in red and brown, respectively. The data show that the impedance-only controller performed well in tracking along the $y$ and $z$ axes, whereas the admittance-only controller showed relatively small CoG position variations along the $x$ axis. In comparison, the hybrid controller maintained competitive performance across all four metrics.


Therefore, although the individual impedance and admittance controllers exhibited advantages in their corresponding directions, the hybrid controller combined these characteristics and simultaneously maintained good performance in both the sliding and contact directions. \subsection{Section Summary}
The experiment highlights the complementary roles of the two strategies: impedance control improves sliding smoothness and reduces tracking error, whereas admittance control enhances adaptive interaction by decreasing both CoG displacement and contact forces. Since neither the impedance-only controller nor the admittance-only controller can achieve both behaviors, the hybrid controller yields smaller trajectory deviations, reduced unintended CoG drift, and lower interaction forces compared with the baseline PD controller, confirming its effectiveness for compliant contact-rich tasks.

\section{Summary}
In this chapter, the proposed hybrid impedance--admittance control framework was experimentally evaluated on multi-link aerial robots. In the preliminary experiments, the parameter adjustment experience was discussed, the performance under free flight was evaluated, and the effects of impedance and admittance control were demonstrated. The Hydrus Xi experiments compared four controllers and demonstrated that the hybrid controller combined the resilience of impedance control with the adaptivity of admittance control, achieving good performance in both the sliding and contact directions. Finally, the extension experiment on DRAGON demonstrated that the proposed framework could also be applied to another type of multi-link aerial robot.
