\chapter{Experiments}
In this chapter, we will demonstrate the effects of the proposed hybrid impedance--admittance control framework. Hydrus Xi is selected as the primary experimental platform, while DRAGON is used as an extension platform. The experiments in this chapter have the following three main objectives. 

First, preliminary experiments are conducted to demonstrate the individual effects of impedance and admittance control on the multi-link aerial robot and to validate their characteristics discussed in Chapters~\ref{ch:impedance} and~\ref{ch:admittance}.

Second, comparative surface sliding experiments on Hydrus Xi are conducted to evaluate the proposed hybrid control framework. The experiments examine whether impedance and admittance control can be combined while maintaining stable surface sliding, whether their respective control effects can be preserved after the combination, and whether the hybrid controller can simultaneously provide the desired behaviors in both the sliding and contact directions, as intended in Chapter~\ref{ch:hybrid}.

Finally, an extension experiment on DRAGON is conducted to demonstrate the feasibility of applying the proposed framework to another type of multi-link aerial robot.

\section{Controller Parameter Adjustment and Individual Control Validation on Hydrus Xi}
\subsection{Practical Parameter-Selection Considerations}
\label{sec:parameter_selection}

In this work, the underactuated multi-link aerial robot Hydrus Xi was used as the primary experimental platform, and its controller parameters were selected according to the following practical considerations. The parameter selection considered the characteristics of the impedance and admittance parameters, the specific experimental conditions and task requirements, and the practical constraints of the experimental platform.

Since Hydrus Xi is an underactuated system, the rotational and translational parameters need to be tuned in a cascaded manner, with the rotational parameters tuned first, followed by the translational parameters. According to the principle mentioned in Sec.~\ref{sec:imp_param_tuning}, the PD parameters were considered first. To achieve fast convergence to the desired orientation, the normalized stiffness parameters $\tilde{k}_d$ in the roll and pitch axes, which correspond to the proportional gains $k_p^{PID}$ in the PID controller, were set to relatively large values. Meanwhile, to avoid oscillation, the damping ratio $\zeta$ in these two axes was tuned to approximately $1.2$--$1.4$. The remaining parameters were then adjusted according to the experimental conditions.

Next, the mass ratio $\mu$ in each direction needs to be taken into account during contact. To reduce the influence of external torques on the orientation, the mass ratio $\mu$ in these directions was set relatively small, typically around $0.1$--$0.2$ in our experiments. For the translational directions, the selection depends on the task requirements. In the surface sliding task, contact is usually required in one direction while sliding occurs in the other two directions, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:hybrid_controller}. In the sliding directions, it is important to resist friction-induced disturbances. Therefore, $\mu$ is set smaller than $1$, typically around $0.3$--$1$ in our experiments. In the contact direction, only admittance behavior is required, while the impedance characteristic is not introduced. Therefore, $\mu$ is set exactly to $1$.

The admittance parameters were also selected based on the following principles.

The admittance stiffness $k_a$ was mainly chosen according to the task requirements. If a large geometric variation is expected, a relatively small stiffness should be selected, and vice versa. Meanwhile, a larger stiffness results in less obvious deformation, whereas a smaller stiffness may introduce significant configuration variations that could lead to instability. Therefore, the admittance stiffness needs to be appropriately tuned according to the actual task conditions.

The admittance mass $m_a$ was selected mainly according to the required response speed. A larger admittance mass results in a slower response, which may lead to insufficient adaptation to relatively fast geometric variations. On the other hand, a smaller admittance mass results in a faster response, which may introduce instability since the configuration may change too rapidly. Overall, the admittance mass also needs to be appropriately tuned according to the task requirements.

Finally, the admittance damping $c_a$ was usually selected together with $m_a$ and $k_a$. The admittance damping ratio $\zeta_a=\frac{c_a}{2\sqrt{m_a k_a}}$ was generally set slightly below $1$ to obtain a response close to critical damping.


\subsection{Free Flight Parameter Tuning}
\label{sec:free_flight}

According to the parameter tuning principle mentioned in Sec.~\ref{sec:imp_param_tuning}, the PD parameters are first tuned under free flight conditions before introducing contact interaction. The parameters were adjusted according to the practical considerations introduced in Sec.~\ref{sec:parameter_selection}. Note that at this stage, the mass ratio $\mu$ in all directions is set to $1$, so that no additional impedance characteristic is introduced. Meanwhile, the admittance controller is deactivated. Therefore, the controller in this experiment can be regarded as the baseline PD controller.

\subsection{Impedance Response under External Force}
\label{sec:impedance_parameter}

In this experiment, the effect of the mass ratio $\mu$ on the response of the multi-link aerial robot to external forces is investigated. As discussed in Chapter~\ref{ch:impedance}, the mass ratio $\mu$ can reshape the behavior of the aerial robot by changing the desired stiffness of the system. In particular, $\mu<1$ provides a more rigid response to external disturbances, whereas $\mu>1$ provides a softer response.

In this experiment, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:imp_drag}, a rope was used to horizontally drag Hydrus Xi and generate an external force along the $x$ axis. Pulling the rope along the positive direction of the $x$ axis can be regarded as applying a positive external force along this direction. The other controller parameters were kept the same as those used in the free flight experiment, as listed in Table~\ref{tb:params1}. Three different mass ratios, $\mu=2$, $1$, and $0.5$, were tested, corresponding to the soft, normal, and rigid cases, respectively. 

Under impedance control, the external force induces a corresponding displacement according to the designed second-order dynamics. Therefore, the relationship between the external force and the CoG position error along the $x$ axis is used to evaluate the response under different mass ratios. Specifically, the slope of the force--position relationship is defined as the effective stiffness, where a larger value indicates that a larger external force is required to produce the same position error.

On the other hand,  when $\mu$ is smaller than $1$, the system can be considered as a soft system.

Therefore, this capability to adjust the system response to external forces provides the basis for applying impedance control in the sliding directions to improve the resistance to friction-induced disturbances.

As discussed in Chapter~\ref{ch:admittance}, the relationship between the external forces and the resulting deformation constitutes one part of the response cycle for adapting to geometric variations. The complete response cycle will be further demonstrated in the surface sliding experiments.

In this experiment, as shown at the bottom of Fig.~\ref{fig:admittance_drag}, a rope was also used to generate an external force along the $x$ axis. Here, the distance $r$ used in the admittance controller in \eqref{eq:admittance control2} is defined as the projected distance between the end effector and the CoG along the $x$ axis. To avoid unexpected configurations and instability, Hydrus Xi started from the nominal configuration shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:admittance_drag}(a), and the distance $r$ was constrained within a feasible range during the experiment.

\section{Comparative Surface-Sliding Evaluation on Hydrus Xi}

In this section, the surface sliding experiment was conducted using Hydrus Xi under the proposed hybrid impedance--admittance control framework. As discussed in Chapter~\ref{ch:hybrid}, impedance and admittance control were combined in their respective directions to utilize their different characteristics. The experiment investigated whether the two control methods can be combined while maintaining stable surface sliding and whether their respective control effects can be preserved after the combination.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
In the surface sliding experiment, the controller parameters were set as follows. The basic PD parameters were kept the same as those listed in Table~\ref{tb:params1}. Based on the discussion in Chapter~\ref{ch:impedance} and the experimental results presented in Sec.~\ref{sec:impedance_parameter}, the mass ratio $\mu$ in each direction was selected according to the experimental requirements. For the orientation, $\mu$ was chosen to provide sufficient resistance to external torques generated during contact and sliding. For the sliding directions, especially the $y$ axis, $\mu$ was selected to provide a more rigid response to external disturbances. Along the contact direction, namely the $x$ axis, the mass ratio $\mu$ was set to $1$. The resulting parameters are summarized in Table~\ref{tb:params4}. Note that the diagonal elements of the mass ratio translational matrix $m\boldsymbol{M}_d^{-1}$ and the mass ratio rotational matrix $\boldsymbol{I}\boldsymbol{I}_d^{-1}$ represent the mass ratios $\mu$ in the corresponding translational and rotational directions, respectively. Meanwhile, the admittance controller was activated only along the $x$ axis, and its parameters were selected based on the practical considerations introduced in Sec.~\ref{sec:parameter_selection}. These parameters are also shown in Table~\ref{tb:params4}. The reference external force used in the admittance controller was set to $f_{ref,x}=0$. The parameters used for external wrench estimation are also summarized in the same table.

Throughout the experiment, a constant yaw angle was maintained by keeping the end effector aligned with the $x$ axis of $\{W\}$. This setting allowed the surface geometric variations to be equivalently represented as variations along the direction of the end effector. Specifically, the configuration angle $\theta$, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:admittance_drag}, was defined as the sole variable determining the configuration in the admittance controller.

Hydrus Xi was required to start from the nominal configuration that is also shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:admittance_drag}. Assuming that each link has a similar mass, this nominal configuration approximately keeps the CoG on the extension line of the end effector, while the other allowable configurations also approximately satisfy this condition. Therefore, the external force applied along the end effector direction passes close to the CoG and generates only a small external torque about the CoG. This setting helps to reduce the potential instability caused by excessive external torque during contact. Under the above configuration settings, the corresponding joint angles were analytically obtained through inverse kinematics as

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:joint angles}
 \begin{bmatrix}
    q_1 \\
    q_2 \\
    q_3
    \end{bmatrix} 
    =
    \begin{bmatrix}
    \frac{\pi}{2}-\theta \\
    2\theta \\
    -\theta
    \end{bmatrix},
\end{equation}


The experimental environment is illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:exp_scene}. A vertical board was placed at a known location within the testing area. The board's surface provided sufficient friction for stable contact while still allowing smooth sliding of the end effector (a marker pen). The surface was intentionally inclined by approximately $20^\circ$ with respect to the $y$ axis, and the inclination angle was kept unknown to the robot. Although the surface was planar, its unknown inclination introduces unmodeled geometric variations that serve a similar role to curved surfaces in this study. The friction coefficient was also unknown. Consequently, both the contact forces and the surface geometry remained unmodeled in this experiment, providing suitable conditions for evaluating the proposed surface sliding control framework.

In the surface sliding experiment, the reference trajectory of the multi-link aerial robot was defined on a plane perpendicular to the $x$ axis. The reference trajectory was defined for the CoG of the aerial robot, while the end effector trajectory was additionally observed to evaluate the actual sliding behavior on the surface. The robot was commanded to follow the predefined circular trajectory with a radius of $0.30\,\mathrm{m}$ and a period of $14\,\mathrm{s}$ within this plane. Due to the board's unknown inclination angle, the desired circular trajectory resulted in approximately $0.15\,\mathrm{m}$ of geometric variation along the $x$ axis during one cycle. This geometric variation was not incorporated into the reference trajectory generation or controller design. Therefore, the robot was required to respond to this geometric variation online during contact.

Note that each control method is executed independently along its respective axes. Overall, Hydrus Xi maintained stable flight and surface contact throughout the sliding task under the hybrid controller. These results demonstrate the feasibility of implementing the proposed hybrid impedance--admittance control framework on Hydrus Xi.

\subsection{Sliding-Direction Tracking and Disturbance Rejection}

Since the $y$ and $z$ axes corresponded to the sliding directions, where friction-induced disturbances were dominant, the CoG tracking errors along these two axes were used to evaluate the sliding performance. Smaller tracking errors indicate better trajectory tracking performance. Fig.~\ref{fig:impedance_plot} presents the CoG trajectories together with the reference path under the four controllers. Although Fig.~\ref{fig:impedance_plot} illustrates the overall trajectories of the four controllers, their performance differences become more evident in the tracking error distributions shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:impedance_box}. In particular, the median tracking errors along the $y$ axis were approximately $0.134\,\mathrm{m}$ for the PD controller, $0.079\,\mathrm{m}$ for the impedance-only controller, $0.105\,\mathrm{m}$ for the admittance-only controller, and $0.083\,\mathrm{m}$ for the hybrid controller.

Overall, this part of the experiments demonstrates that impedance control provided a resilient response to friction-induced disturbances in the sliding directions, enabling more accurate sliding without requiring explicit friction values while still approximately following the desired trajectory. This characteristic was also maintained after combining impedance control with admittance control.




\subsection{Contact-Direction Adaptation and Interaction Force}

Fig.~\ref{fig:admittance_plot} presents the CoG position and external force along the $x$ axis. The interquartile range of the CoG position was used to evaluate how strongly the CoG position was affected by the surface geometric variations, with a smaller value indicating a smaller influence. As further quantified in Fig.~\ref{fig:admittance_box}, the PD and impedance-only controllers showed relatively larger variations in the CoG position, with interquartile ranges of $0.133\,\mathrm{m}$ and $0.137\,\mathrm{m}$, respectively. In contrast, the interquartile ranges were $0.088\,\mathrm{m}$ and $0.084\,\mathrm{m}$ for the admittance-only and hybrid controllers, respectively.

The median external force along the $x$ axis was used to evaluate the overall influence of the surface interaction on the CoG, where a median value closer to zero indicates a smaller influence. As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:admittance_box}, the median external forces were approximately $-1.190\,\mathrm{N}$ for the PD controller, $-0.453\,\mathrm{N}$ for the impedance-only controller, $-0.712\,\mathrm{N}$ for the admittance-only controller, and $-0.328\,\mathrm{N}$ for the hybrid controller. 

Compared with the baseline PD controller, the admittance-only controller showed a smaller interquartile range in the CoG position. In the baseline PD case, the configuration of the multi-link aerial robot remained unchanged, and therefore the variation in the CoG position approximately followed the geometric variation of the surface, which was around $0.15\,\mathrm{m}$. In contrast, the admittance-only controller showed a smaller variation in the CoG position, as indicated by its smaller interquartile range, due to the deformation of the multi-link structure. The surface geometric variation first induced changes in the external force, which further caused structural deformation under admittance control, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:admittance_plot}. This deformation reduced the geometric variation transmitted to the CoG. This response demonstrates the complete response cycle discussed in Chapter~\ref{ch:admittance} and its capability to adapt to surface geometric variations.

\section{Summary}
In this chapter, the proposed hybrid impedance--admittance control framework was experimentally evaluated on multi-link aerial robots. In the preliminary experiments, practical parameter-selection considerations were discussed, stable operation under free flight conditions was demonstrated, and the effects of impedance and admittance control were investigated. The Hydrus Xi experiments compared four controllers and demonstrated that the hybrid controller combined the resilience of impedance control with the adaptivity of admittance control, achieving good performance in both the sliding and contact directions.
