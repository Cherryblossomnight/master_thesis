\section{Research Background and Objective}
Recently, aerial manipulation has attracted a lot of attention in the robotic research field \cite{ollero_2021_past}. Compared with conventional ground robot manipulation, aerial manipulation emphasizes that the manipulator could reach high places or enter narrow spaces, which the legged or wheeled robots have trouble reaching. Naturally, the tasks like aerial transportation, high-altitude contact and disaster relief can be executed. Current aerial manipulation platforms have achieved a variety of tasks.

However, when facing a broader range of aerial manipulation tasks, both mechanical structures and control algorithms of current aerial manipulation platforms are insufficient. Here we introduce a representative interaction task named the contact-rich surface sliding task, in which an aerial robot is typically required to maintain a pressing contact between its end-effector and an unknown uneven surface while following a desired sliding trajectory. Surface sliding tasks are commonly derived from scenarios like infrastructure surface inspection and cleaning \cite{bodie_2020_active, trujillo_2019_novel, tognon_2019_truly}, which means they are very common and significant task scenarios. Such tasks inherently involve a coupling between unmodeled surface geometry and frictional effects, leading to significant uncertainties in both the external wrench and the contact geometry experienced by the robot. Therefore, as the contact interaction situation varies during sliding, to complete the task requirement, both the force and position conditions need to be considered simultaneously. Consequently, the platform used for surface sliding tasks must exhibit both robustness and compliant behavior, enabling the end-effector to remain resilient to force disturbances while adapting its relative configuration to changing contact conditions. To achieve these, we need to consider areas for improvement in both mechanical structures and control algorithms.

% The aerial robot part is mainly used for locomotion and the manipulator part usually used for manipulation. Such principle of designing has two benefits. First it helps reducing the complexity of control logic since the locomotion and manipulation part are separated. Second, the manipulator part can be replaced according to the task requirements, which means all of morphologies can be realized on a same aerial robot by simply exchanging the manipulators.

We first consider potential improvements to the mechanical structure. Intuitively, aerial manipulation tasks can be executed by an aerial robot equipped with a manipulator. Therefore, current aerial morphologies are designed by either attaching a static manipulator (e.g., a tool or a stick) to the aerial robot, or mounting a robotic arm on the aerial robot. In both cases mentioned above, the whole aerial mechanical system simply consists of the aerial robot itself and the manipulator. Until now, the design principle has been proven effective across various tasks. However, when the complexity of the task increases, such traditional morphologies may suffer from scenarios that require a higher degree of freedom and operation in confined spaces. It is necessary to consider developing aerial robots with other morphologies.

Considering the complex requirements of real-world environments, in order to raise the performance ceiling of existing aerial robots for aerial manipulation, articulated multi-link aerial robots were developed. Multi-link aerial robots consist of articulated serial-link structures, which enables them to achieve serpentine flying trajectory and undergo significant deformation in mid-air. Besides, their deformability provides additional degrees of freedom for manipulation during flight \cite{zhao_2021_singularity, zhao_2018_design, yang_2018_lasdra, hameed_2025_dragonfly}. Their structures help to go through narrow spaces or enhance the operation wrench \cite{zhao_2020_online, zhao_2022_forceful}, which significantly expands the range of methods available for aerial manipulation. Owing to these structural advantages, multi-link aerial robots offer opportunities for many applications, including inspection and search-and-rescue tasks. Their effectiveness has been demonstrated in a variety of experimental tasks, such as grasping objects \cite{hameed_2025_dragonfly, shi_2020_aerial, zhao_2023_versatile}, opening sliding doors \cite{sugito_2022_aerial} and manipulating valves \cite{zhao_2022_forceful}. Inspired by the success of deformable structures in various aerial manipulation tasks, such robots can adapt to potential geometric variations, making this morphology a promising solution for the proposed surface sliding task.

Secondly, regarding control strategies, force control plays an essential role in contact-rich tasks. Two widely adopted force control approaches, impedance control and admittance control, have been extensively applied in the aerial manipulation tasks \cite{ollero_2021_past}. However, they each have their own advantages and disadvantages. Impedance control performs well in regulating interaction forces and resisting external force disturbances, but typically exhibits limited adaptability to changing contact geometry. In contrast, admittance control is effective in adapting to geometric variations, but is generally less robust against large interaction force disturbances. These differences originate from the complementary yet opposite causal relationships of impedance and admittance control paradigms \cite{ott_2010_unified}. Consequently, contact-rich tasks that simultaneously require disturbance rejection and adaptation to uncertain surface geometry cannot be reliably addressed by either approach alone, naturally motivating a combination of both control principles to achieve resilient and adaptive interaction capabilities.

Previous researches has paid a lot of attention to impedance control or admittance control for aerial robot. However, currently, there is no a control method to combine them together, while obviously we need the capabilities of both. This is because achieving such a combination is not trivial. Due to the opposite causality of impedance and admittance control, a single actuation source cannot independently realize both interaction objectives and generally requires additional coordination mechanisms \cite{ott_2010_unified}. Conventional multi-rotor aerial robot platforms, which rely exclusively on rotor thrusts, inherently possess only a single actuation source and are therefore limited in their ability to simultaneously exploit the complementary properties of the two paradigms.

If we wish to achieve the impedance and admittance controller on the same platform, it is necessary to consider a new structure that has more than a single actuation source. Although some aerial manipulation platforms (e.g., \cite{lippiello_2012_cartesian, cataldi_2016_impedance}) introduce joint actuation and therefore provide an additional actuation source, existing studies have not leveraged this structural characteristic to simultaneously employ both impedance and admittance control. This limitation further motivates the design of our control strategy. To simultaneously realize impedance and admittance control, we employ the multi-link aerial robots that provide an additional actuation source through their articulated joints.

In this work, we propose a novel hybrid impedance--admittance control framework enabled by multi-link aerial robots, which exploits the two independent actuation sources of the robot by assigning them distinct control roles. In particular, rotor thrusts govern the global motion of the platform, whereas the joint actuators regulate the end-effector pose relative to the platform's body. This structure enables impedance control to be implemented through rotor thrusts and admittance control through joint actuation, allowing both paradigms to be realized simultaneously on a single aerial platform. Within the proposed framework, impedance control regulates the aerial robot's center-of-gravity (CoG) motion to achieve disturbance-resilient interaction, while admittance control adjusts joint angles to accommodate unmodeled surface geometric and contact-induced variations. Through this division of roles, the proposed strategy enables resilient and adaptive sliding along unknown surfaces. The task execution process is illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:main_figure}.

\section{Previous Research}

\subsection{Surface Sliding Aerial Manipulation Tasks}

Aerial manipulation tasks, as introduced before, play an important role in the development of aerial robots. Here, the surface sliding task is one of the most challenging tasks. In this work, we need to deal with the unmodeled surface which may introduce uncertainties in force and geometry. Similar conditions and some solutions can be found in previous research.

Based on the existing research on surface sliding, we can find that this kind of task has the following characteristics: (a) the end effector of the aerial robot needs to maintain contact with the surface for a long time; (b) the end effector of the aerial robot needs to generate tangential motion related to the surface. Meanwhile, the real environments bring the following difficulties to the tasks: (c) the contact force, including both tangential and normal forces, may influence the performance of the aerial robot; (d) the surface geometry may be complex and unknown in the real world, while the friction condition and geometry may also change during the task. Considering the above features, the surface sliding tasks require the aerial robots to deal with external force disturbances and varying contact conditions. 

In the existing literature, aerial robots have been developed to deal with the real-world requirements. Typical applications include bridge inspection \cite{bodie_2020_active}, infrastructure inspection \cite{trujillo_2019_novel}, tube inspection \cite{tognon_2019_truly} and aerial writing \cite{guo_2024_flying} as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:surface_sliding}. These tasks usually need to deal with curved or soft surfaces while maintaining contact and sliding along the surface. Therefore, the disturbances brought by uncertain contact conditions must be taken into account.

Currently, although many studies have paid attention to surface sliding tasks, the main features of this kind of manipulation task have not been systematically summarized, and an effective framework to address the above challenges has not been clearly established. Therefore, further development of both the aerial robot structure and the control method is required to deal with the challenges in the surface sliding tasks.

\subsection{Morphologies of Aerial Robot for Aerial Manipulation}
% Considering that the aeria manipulation is directly derived from the ground manipulation field, the morphology of the manipulatiors attached in the aerial robot is usually very similar with those in the ground robot. Obviously, we can used the mechanical design and control method that have been demonstrated in the ground robot field.
%The morphology is very important for the aerial manipulation\cite{ollero_2021_past}. 
We can briefly divide the morphologies of aerial manipulation into four types: single rigid body morphology, parallel morphology, serial morphology and other specific morphologies like multi-link aerial robots, as illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:morphologies}. These morphologies have their own features for different types of tasks.

The single rigid body morphology is defined as mounting a single rigid body manipulator such as a rod or a stick on the aerial robot, and the manipulator usually keeps static relative to the aerial robot. The mechanical structure and the control method of this morphology is relatively simple, so it has been used early and widely.
By mainly controlling the aerial robot itself to provide degrees of freedom for the end effector, the rigid manipulator is used for tasks like push-and-slide and peg-in-hole. 
Recent research using this kind of morphology mostly pays attention to the control method, therefore uses the simple morphology in order to reduce the complexity. For example, Bodie et al. \cite{bodie_2020_active} used a static end effector to inspect bridges. Brunner et al. \cite{brunner_2022_planning} realized using a rod with a hook to operate an articulated door. 
However, for more complicated tasks, the rigid manipulator may not be enough. Meanwhile, the rigid manipulator do not have extra DoFs, which limits its capability when additional motion is required. Considering these limitations of the single rigid body morphology, we did not use it in our work.


The parallel morphology is defined as an aerial robot mounted with a delta manipulator. Since the delta manipulator has the advantages of low inertia, high stiffness and fast response due to its mechanical structure, the parallel morphology can realize high-speed motion and accurate position control. 
Due to these advantages, a few researchers have used this kind of morphology. Guo et al. \cite{guo_2024_flying} performed calligraphy on a board with the parallel morphology, showing the potential of this morphology for precise manipulation tasks. Deng et al. \cite{deng_2025_whole} realized high-speed manipulation like pushing objects and grasping object, indicating the ability to achieve fast motion requirements.
The above applications demonstrate that the parallel morphology can perform well in high-precision and high-speed requirements. However, since the surface sliding tasks usually require large workspace that the delta manipulator does not has, this morphology will struggle when facing a wider range of surface geometric variations.

The serial morphology is defined as an aerial robot mounted with an robotic arm manipulaton. The serial structure of the robotic arm provides large workspace and flexible motion. In contrast to the parallel morphology, the serial morphology is usually used in tasks requiring large workspace and obstacle avoidance ability. 
Mounting a robotic arm on an aerial robot is an intuitive idea, and therefore this morphology has been widely investigated. He et al. \cite{he_2025_flying} used a serial arm to draw a relatively large figure on a board. 
Lee et al. \cite{lee_2025_autonomous} realized SE(3) operation like a peg-in-hole task in a large scenario. 
These studies demonstrated that the serial morphology provides strong manipulation capabilities and potential flexibility.
Due to the multi-DoF structure, both the serial morphology and multi-link morphology provide flexible manipulation capabilities for aerial robots. However, unlike a robotic arm mounted on an aerial platform, the multi-link morphology integrates the aerial platform and articulated structure together, providing a different way to utilize structural deformation for aerial manipulation.


%Too support the hole arm, it needs high quality rotor, which will also bring high weight.

The multi-link aerial robot itself can be called as a multi-link morphology. This morphology can be regarded as a flying robotic arm, so it shares several advantages with the serial morphology. Meanwhile, the multi-link aerial robots can deform their structures to pass through narrow gaps that the conventional aerial robots can not achieve.
The extra DoFs of the multi-link aerial robots can also help them realize more complex manipulation. Sugito et al. \cite{sugito_2022_aerial} used a multi-link aerial robot to push a door. Shi et al. \cite{shi_2020_aerial} used a multi-link aerial robot to grasp a box. We already know the morphology with serial structures has the potential for surface sliding tasks. However, the multi-link aerial robot has not yet been used in the surface sliding tasks. Therefore in this work, considering the morphology features, we develop the control framework for the multi-link aerial robots.


In conclusion, the morphologies of aerial robots influence their abilities: parallel morphology is usually used for agile manipulation tasks, while the serial morphology is used for tasks requiring a large workspace. When it comes to complicated aerial manipulation tasks like surface sliding tasks, a multi-link aerial robot is a good choice.


\subsection{Interaction Control Method Used in Aerial Manipulation}
For increasingly complex conditions that the contact-rich aerial manipulation tasks need to deal with, it is important to introduce force-control methods. Previous work has commonly adopted force-control strategies such as impedance control and admittance control \cite{ollero_2021_past}. 

Both impedance and admittance control can be considered as general impedance control \cite{valency_2003_accuracy}. The concept of impedance control was proposed by Hogan \cite{hogan_1984_impedance}. In his work, the environment should be considered as an admittance; therefore, if the robot performs like an impedance, a reasonable interaction relationship can be realized. 

Currently, impedance and admittance control are usually distinguished according to their different causalities \cite{valency_2003_accuracy, ott_2010_unified}. Impedance control uses the motion error as the input and determines the corresponding force command as the response, while admittance control takes the interaction force as the input and generates the corresponding motion response. Due to this difference, impedance control is more naturally applied to systems with force actuators, while admittance control is commonly realized by utilizing position actuators or used as an outer-loop filter to provide reference position commands for lower-level controllers.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/chapter1/force_control.png}
  \caption{Some example tasks that use impedance or admittance control. (a) The impedance control adjusts the impedance gains according to surface proximity for surface sliding \cite{bodie_2020_active}. (b) The impedance parameters are tuning by the learning-based method, which helps the aerial robot achieve sliding on an uneven surface \cite{zhang_2022_learning}. (c) The admittance controller is used as filter to adapt to the external disturbances \cite{ryll_2019_6d}. (d) The admittance control is applied to a robotic arm to achieve sliding on a curved surface \cite{solanes_2018_adaptive}.}
  \label{fig:force_control}
\end{figure}

Among these strategies, impedance control has been extensively explored for aerial robots. 
In the early stage of aerial manipulation research, the impedance control method was widely used \cite{ollero_2021_past}. Forte et al. \cite{forte_2012_impedance} developed a 3D impedance controller for a single rigid-body aerial robot, and Lippiello et al. \cite{lippiello_2012_cartesian} proposed an impedance controller for an aerial robot with a serial robotic arm. After these works introduced impedance control into aerial manipulation tasks, later studies focused more on the interaction behaviors that impedance control can realize.

In recent years, impedance control has been used in more complicated tasks.
Bodie et al. \cite{bodie_2020_active} introduced a six-DoF axis-selective impedance controller capable of adjusting impedance gains based on surface proximity, which realized sliding along the surface while following a specific trajectory and can be used for bridge inspection tasks. Zhang et al. \cite{zhang_2022_learning} employed learning-based techniques to adapt the impedance parameters, which help the aerial robot slide on an uneven plane. Both works paid attention to using impedance control as an adjustment mechanism to adapt to unknown external environments with different interaction-force requirements.


The application of impedance control to aerial robots has become relatively mature. However, current literature still mainly focuses on designing useful control frameworks instead of analyzing how the impedance controller performs under different interaction conditions, or how its parameters affect the performance of aerial manipulation. Meanwhile, These methods can effectively respond to external force disturbances through gain modulation, but there is limited evidence that they remain effective in environments with uneven or irregular surfaces by adapting to the geometric variations. 


In addition to impedance approaches, admittance control has also been applied in aerial manipulation tasks. In some studies, the admittance control was utilized as an outer-loop filter, while some traditional position controllers such as PID controller are used as the inner controller. Ryll et al. \cite{ryll_2019_6d} applied admittance control by enabling an aerial robot to follow variations in the contact-plane geometry, while the induced lateral force disturbances caused noticeable rotational deviations. Cataldi et al. \cite{cataldi_2016_impedance} applied an admittance filter to calculate the reference for the lower-level controller. Compared with the impedance control, the admittance control has been less widely used in the aerial manipulation tasks.


In contrast to its relatively limited application to aerial robots,admittance control has been widely used for robotic manipulators to generate compliant motion during physical interaction. For example, Solanes et al. \cite{solanes_2018_adaptive} applied admittance control to command the joint angles, in response to the interaction with a curved surface. In our framework, admittance control is applied to the multi-link structure as a regulator to apapt to external geometric variations by joint motion. The mechanism is therefore more similar to controlling a robotic manipulator than controlling the aerial robot itself, making methods from the robotic manipulator control field more relevant to our framework.

In conclusion, while these studies demonstrate the potential of impedance- or admittance-based approaches for aerial manipulation tasks in interaction scenarios, each method inevitably inherits the limitations of its underlying control principle.
This thesis tries to combine these two controllers together on the same platform in order to exploit their respective strengths in dealing with different interaction requirements.

\section{The Construction of the Thesis}

Based on the related research and current problems introduced above, this thesis investigates force control methods for multi-link aerial robots, with a particular focus on contact-rich surface sliding tasks. The overall structure of the thesis is organized as follows.

\begin{itemize}
  \item Chapter 1 introduces the research background and reviews related work about the definition and solution of surface sliding tasks, morphologies of aerial robots and force-control methods used in aerial manipulation. Based on the above discussion, the motivation, objectives, and proposed approach of this thesis are introduced.
  \item Chapter 2 presents the preliminary materials of this thesis, including the notation definitions, the modeling of multi-link aerial robots and the external wrench estimation algorithm.
  \item Chapter 3 introduces the impedance control for CoG motion, analyzes how impedance control can redesign the dynamic parameters of a system, and provides a new insight into the impedance parameters. The implementation on multi-link aerial robots is also introduced.
  \item Chapter 4 introduces the admittance control for joint motion. Admittance control is used to adapt to external geometric variations through structural deformation by adjusting the configuration of the aerial robot.
  \item Chapter 5 introduces how to combine impedance control and admittance control as hybrid impedance--admittance control in the same system. The effects of the integrated hybrid controller are explained.
  \item Chapter 6 presents the experimental results, discusses the controller parameters, and exhibits the preliminary experiments on the impedance and admittance responses to external forces. Hydrus Xi is selected to demonstrate the main concepts, while DRAGON is used to show the applicability of the proposed framework to different multi-link aerial robots.
  \item Finally, Chapter 7 concludes the thesis by summarizing the main findings of this work and outlining directions for future research.
\end{itemize}
